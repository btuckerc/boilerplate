# Voice reference

## Provenance and confidence

Created at Tucker’s explicit request following a September 20–21, 2026 social
writing session. The primary evidence was a bounded selection of his recent
user-authored OMP messages about Dictation, StreamApp, Usage Bar and public
project planning. An extraction identified 92 substantial user-role messages
from September 20 03:51 UTC through September 21 01:47 UTC; selected examples
were read, not statistically modeled. Assistant replies, tool output and injected
instructions were not treated as his voice.

The earlier S3 prose review in the `btuckercdev-profile` workspace supported
concrete situations, contractions, short sentences, no em dashes, no slogans,
and preserving meaning without copying every hedge. That assistant-written
review was secondary evidence. The workspace’s `SOCIAL-DRAFTING-FORMULAS.md`
contains the fuller private analysis; this reference does not depend on it.

These are recent text samples from a period when Tucker was using Dictation,
not a complete archive since its creation. Individual messages cannot all be
identified as dictated. No audio was analyzed. This is a revisable editorial
interpretation, not a perfect or permanent model of his speech.

## What to preserve

**Personal reason, then the concrete thing.** Tucker often explains what he wants
to do before describing the mechanism. “I want to be able to share them” is more
useful than an abstract claim about productivity. Do not force a personal origin
story when the task is simply to answer a question or explain a fact.

**Connected, ordinary sentences.** He often develops an explanation with “so,”
“and,” “but,” or “Oh, also.” Keep that continuity. Mix short statements with a
longer explanation rather than turning every sentence into a polished punchline.
Do not begin every paragraph with the same phrase.

**Concrete preferences and observations.** Say which button, setting, result or
interaction matters and why. Prefer “press the button and circle what I mean”
to “activate the annotation workflow.” Technical terms can stay when they are
necessary; explain rather than replace them with a metaphor.

**Honest uncertainty.** “I think,” “I’m wondering,” and “I’d like to” can express
real judgment or intention. Keep them where they carry meaning. Do not hedge a
verified fact or turn an intention into a guaranteed delivery date.

**Specific enthusiasm without overselling.** It is fine to be excited about how
something looks or works. Describe the actual thing. Avoid “game-changing,”
“seamless,” invented superlatives, competitor takedowns, and vague promises.

**Directness when something is unnecessary.** Clear, simple requests and
explanations fit better than padded positioning. Do not confuse his dislike of
unnecessary UI prose with permission to remove essential public disclosures.

## What not to imitate

- Repeated starts, obvious recognition errors, accidental duplication or tangled
  dictation punctuation. Preserve the intended meaning; do not guess unclear facts.
- A quota of “like,” “you know,” “yea,” “basically,” or “if that makes sense.”
  These may occur naturally, but deliberately adding them is caricature.
- Profanity from private troubleshooting as a default public persona.
- Every sentence beginning “I wanted” or “I’ve been working on.”
- Assistant-style slogans, noun-heavy feature summaries and forced three-part
  taglines. Adding a first-person pronoun does not fix a product-description voice.
- Em dashes in public prose, following the earlier editorial preference. Use
  periods, commas or parentheses when they read naturally; this is not a ban on
  ordinary hyphens in technical names.
- Current app names, product limits or posting dates as permanent voice rules.
  Examples below illustrate wording only; verify facts for the actual draft.

## Example transformations

These are proposed editorial examples, not quotations, approved posts, or current
product verification. Do not paste them into unrelated drafts.

### Product description → personal explanation

Before: “Technical dictation on my Mac, built on Handy.”

After: “I’ve been using Dictation to talk through what I want to build instead
of typing it all out. I wanted to show how it handles some of the technical
stuff I’m saying.”

The longer version needs room and a supported first-person use claim. Keep the
project’s credit and privacy explanation elsewhere in the same appropriate
caption or disclosure; style is not permission to omit them.

### Stiff disclosure → ordinary language

Before: “Optional cleanup sends the transcript to a configured endpoint.”

After: “If you turn cleanup on, the text gets sent to the endpoint you’ve configured.”

Same boundary, more natural sentence. Do not replace it with “everything stays
private” or imply local-only behavior.

### Generic teaser → a specific intention

Before: “More exciting projects coming soon.”

After: “Oh, and I’ve also been working on Dictation. I’d like to show how that
handles the technical stuff I’m saying.”

Use only if that is actually the next subject under consideration. Finish the
current explanation first. No required teaser or “follow for more” sign-off.

### Spoken walkthrough

“I’ve been working on a lot of projects, and I want to be able to share them.
I just wanted the recording setup to be simple.”

Then show the controls and explain the action. Leave room for the actual
interaction instead of filling every second with narration. A private recorded
read-through, not these text samples, establishes the timing.
